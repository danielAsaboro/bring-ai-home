## Spoken script

### Part 1 — Something is happening to intelligence

```
Something is happening to intelligence.

For most of human history, it belonged to people. Then it belonged to institutions—to universities, laboratories, and companies with the money to gather experts and build machines.

Now intelligence is becoming software.

It can read. It can see. It can hear. It can write code, search through a lifetime of documents, operate tools, and reason across problems that once demanded an entire team.

For the first time, ordinary people can put that intelligence inside the products they build.

But there is a catch.
```

### Part 2 — Rented intelligence

```
Most of this new intelligence does not belong to us.

We visit it through an API. We send our questions, documents, voices, photographs—and sometimes the deepest context of our lives—to somebody else’s machine. We pay for every thought and trust that the price, the policy, the model, and the company will still be there tomorrow.

We call it artificial intelligence. But the way most of us use it today is closer to rented intelligence.

And intelligence you rent can be switched off.

A price changes. A rate limit appears. A network fails. A model is retired. A country is blocked. An account is suspended. A company decides that the product you built no longer fits inside its terms.

Nothing about your application may have changed—and yet its brain is gone.

This is not simply a privacy problem. It is an ownership problem.
```

### Part 3 — The people who bring intelligence home

```
The next generation of builders will divide into two groups.

Some will build on borrowed intelligence: products whose memory lives on somebody else’s servers, whose economics are controlled by somebody else’s pricing page, and whose survival depends on somebody else continuing to say yes.

And there will be those who bring intelligence home.

They will build software that can think where the user already is—on the phone in their hand, the laptop on their desk, and the devices they trust. Their products will still work when the internet disappears. Private context will not need to leave the device before it can become useful.

That is the promised land: intelligence that is personal, private, available, and owned.

Not a chatbot trapped inside one window.

An intelligence that can understand your files without uploading them. Hear your voice without sending it away. See through your camera without turning your life into somebody else’s dataset. Use tools with your permission. And, when one device is not powerful enough, reach across a trusted network to another device that is.

Your context.

Your hardware.

Your intelligence.
```

### Part 4 — Why now

```
Until recently, that future sounded better than it worked. Local models were too weak. Consumer hardware was too constrained. The developer experience was fragmented. You could spend days assembling runtimes and fighting native dependencies, only to build a demo that collapsed when you moved it from a laptop to a phone.

But the ground has shifted.

Models have become smaller without becoming useless. The hardware in our pockets and on our desks has become capable of serious inference. Open models now cover language, speech, vision, embeddings, image generation, and more.

The question is no longer whether useful AI can run locally.

The question is what we are going to build now that it can.
```

### Part 5 — Where QVAC enters the story

```
This is where QVAC enters the story.

QVAC is a local-first AI stack for building across devices people already own. It gives JavaScript and TypeScript builders a common way to work with language, retrieval, speech, vision, agents, and more—without making a cloud model the compulsory brain of the product.

And when one device is not enough, the answer does not have to be a centralized server. Devices can cooperate. A phone can remain the interface while a stronger machine handles heavier work. Intelligence can become a network of hardware you trust—not a distant endpoint you are merely allowed to call.

QVAC is not the promised land by itself. It is a set of tools for crossing the obstacles between the world we have and the world we want: open models, local execution, one development surface across devices, and a path from one machine to a private mesh.
```

### Part 6 — Proof, not slides

```
And I know this is more than a slide deck because I built on it.

I’m Daniel, and I built Leash: a private AI system designed to live across your own devices, with local memory, retrieval, voice, vision, tools, and delegated compute—without putting a cloud AI provider in the middle of every thought.

Leash won the QVAC Unleash AI Hackathon.

That does not mean every problem is solved. It means the thesis survived contact with reality. I had to confront memory limits, hardware differences, unreliable outputs, security boundaries, and the uncomfortable distance between an exciting demo and a product somebody can trust.

This series is the map I wish I had before I started.
```

### Part 7 — The journey ahead

```
Over the next ten episodes, we are going to move from the idea to the machinery.

We will make the case for QVAC and examine why this moment is different.

We will build the mental model: how models, runtimes, tasks, devices, and networks fit together.

Then we will stop talking and run our first model.

We will give it private memory through retrieval; senses through speech, vision, and OCR; and agency through tools, skills, and carefully designed permissions.

We will connect devices so a product can become larger than the machine in front of you.

Then I will open Leash and show how those pieces fit inside a real system—what worked, what failed, and what I would build differently today.

Finally, we will look at what comes next: what you can ship, where you can contribute, and what it means to own the intelligence inside your products.
```

### Part 8 — Who this is for

```
This series is for builders.

You do not need a research lab or a rack of GPUs. If you know JavaScript or TypeScript, build for web or mobile, and are willing to look beneath the chat box, you can follow this journey.

I will not promise that local AI replaces every cloud model tomorrow.

I will not hide the trade-offs.

We will test claims on real hardware, work offline, inspect where the data goes, and distinguish what exists now from what is still coming.

Because the goal is not to win an argument about where AI should run.

The goal is to build something useful that still belongs to the person using it.
```
